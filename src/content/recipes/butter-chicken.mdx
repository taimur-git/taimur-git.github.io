## Notes

**Tomato:** swapped tinned passata for fresh tomato, blended raw. Passata means an imported can and a
superstore trip in Dhaka; ripe tomato is a bazar staple. Most fresh-tomato versions of this dish
simmer the tomato down, blend it, then strain it: three extra steps and a sieve. Blending it raw
before it goes in skips all of that; the 12-15 minute simmer cooks out the raw taste just the same.

**Cream:** "double cream" isn't sold here. Any whipping cream or a local brand's cooking cream
(Nestlé, Igloo, or similar) does the job. If it's thick, loosen with a splash of milk.

**Kasuri methi:** the one ingredient on this list that genuinely needs a special-aisle or online
order (Chaldal, Daraz, or a bigger superstore's spice section). It's also what separates this from a
tomato-cream curry rather than a garnish. Worth the one trip; nothing else on this list is.

**Spice bloom and finishing butter:** Joshua Weissman's version stirs its ground spices into the hot
fat for a minute before any liquid hits the pan, rather than adding them with the tomato. More
fragrant for no extra cost. It also holds back a knob of butter to stir in off-heat after the cream,
for gloss and a tighter emulsion, on top of the butter used earlier to cook the onion. Both folded in
here; neither needs a new ingredient, just using the existing butter in two stages.

**Cardamom and green chilli, optional:** Chet Sharma's version blooms whole cardamom (plus cinnamon
and cloves) in the fat, and adds fresh green chilli to the sauce. Left both optional rather than core:
whole garam masala spices are already in most Bangladeshi kitchens for polao and biryani, and green
chilli is a fridge staple, so there's no shopping cost to adding them, but the recipe is not worse
without them.

**Sugar, optional, or gur:** the 2 tsp is there to pull the acid off fresh tomato, which runs sharper
and more variable than passata. A properly ripe bazar tomato in season needs none, so taste before you
add anything. If it needs it, gur does the job as well or better; khejur gur in winter, regular gur
otherwise. Same 2 tsp, melted in a splash of hot water first so it does not sit in a lump. It reads
slightly smoky against the kasuri methi, which suits the dish. Honey works but not here: a 15-minute
simmer cooks off the part you are paying for. If honey is all you have, use 1.5 tsp and stir it in off
the heat at the end with the lemon.

**Cut, not kept:** cashews blended into the sauce, a common way to get a smooth sauce without grating
the onion, but the grated-onion trick here already gets there with no blender and no
soak-then-blend-then-strain step. Adding cashews on top would be two ways of solving the same problem.
Also skipped: mustard oil (ghee already does the sear), Kashmiri-specific chilli powder (colour only,
regular chilli powder is fine), amchur, malt vinegar, kewra water, khoya, and stovetop
charcoal-smoking (dhungar): real flavour, but a second piece of equipment for a finishing touch, not
the dish itself.
