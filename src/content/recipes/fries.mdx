## Shopping

- **Potatoes, 1kg.** Russet and Agria do not exist here and the name on the sack will not tell you
  what you need. Test instead: dissolve 100g salt in 1 litre of water and drop them in. The ones that
  sink are the starchy ones, so buy those. Floaters are waxy and fry up dense and greasy. Diamant is
  the common commercial variety and is bred for processing, so it is a good bet. Skip deshi alu.
- **Oil, 1 to 1.5L.** Soybean or sunflower. A karai needs less than a straight-sided pot.
- **Beresta.** For the blend. Buy a packet or fry your own.
- **Citric acid (tatri).** Any grocery or spice shop.
- **Chicken stock cubes.** Knorr or Maggi.

## Equipment

A deep pot or karai. A spider or slotted spoon. A thermometer if you have one, and there is a test
below if you do not.

## Check the stock cube

Everything else here is potato, oil and spice, so the stock cubes are the only thing on this list
that can carry something you were not expecting. Formulations vary by brand and by market (celery
and wheat both turn up in them), and the cubes also mean these are not vegetarian, even though
nothing else in the recipe is animal. Read the packet, and swap in a vegetable cube if that matters.

## On the blend

The sugar is optional. It is 2 tsp in a 100g jar and the blend works without it, so leave it out if
you want. Gur is not worth chasing here: anything wet turns a dry blend into a brick within a week
and you lose the months of shelf life.

**One teaspoon of tatri, no more.** It is what makes you keep reaching for another chip, and it turns
sharp and metallic fast if you overdo it.

The blend already has salt in it. Add the blend first, taste one, then add plain salt only if it
needs it.

## No thermometer

Drop the handle of a wooden spoon into the oil.

- At 160°C, small bubbles rise steadily and lazily around it.
- At 190°C, the bubbles come fast and vigorously, and the oil shimmers and moves on its own.

If a chip browns in under a minute on the first fry, the oil is too hot; pull the pot off the heat
for a minute. If it just sits there barely bubbling, it is too cold and the chips are drinking oil.

## On the brine

The amounts and the sequence are in the **Brined** component above. What follows is the part that
does not fit in a table.

### Judging the ferment

The source recipes say 3 to 5 days at room temperature. That is written for a 20°C kitchen and it
does not transfer. At 28 to 32°C a lacto ferment runs two to three times faster, which is why the
component says 36 to 48 hours — but go by the signs, not the clock.

Normal looks like a cloudy, slightly milky brine, small bubbles when you nudge the jar, and a smell
that is sour, tangy and a bit yeasty. Like sauerkraut.

- **A thin white film on the surface** is kahm yeast. Harmless. Skim it and carry on.
- **Fuzzy mould — green, black, blue or pink — means throw the whole batch away**, brine and potatoes
  together. Do not scoop it off and use what is underneath; the threads run deeper than what you can
  see. A putrid smell, as opposed to a sour one, means the same.

Two rules prevent almost every failure: keep everything under the brine, and never seal the jar.
Fermentation makes CO2 and a sealed jar builds pressure. Use plain salt, not iodised — iodine holds
the ferment back.

**If you would rather not judge it, ferment in the fridge for 5 to 7 days.** Much slower, much harder
to get wrong, and you get less funk, which some people prefer anyway. This is the one to pick the
first time.

### What you get

Denser and a little less fluffy than the plain ones. Fermentation softens the structure, so they fry
up more solid. That is the trade, not a mistake: the plain version is about texture and this one is
about flavour, so it is the more interesting chip and the less impressive one. Make the plain version
when there are guests.

Beef dripping suits these particularly well — see the note below.

The brine is single use. Throw it out, do not run a second batch through it.

### Without the wait

Brine for **4 hours in the fridge** instead of 48 at room temperature. You get potatoes seasoned all
the way through without any of the sourness, and no judgement calls. It is not the recipe you wanted,
but it is a genuinely good chip and it is the same day. Fry it exactly as the component says, freeze
included.


## From frozen

A real option, not a consolation. Golden Harvest, Pran or imported McCain.

1. Heat the oil to 190°C.
2. Fry from frozen, in small batches, 3 to 4 minutes, until the bubbling dies down and they are
   golden and crisp.
3. Salt and blend immediately.

Ten minutes, and the blend is doing most of the work regardless. Do not thaw them first. They go
soggy and they spit.

## Notes

- **Small batches.** Too many chips at once drops the oil temperature and they absorb fat instead of
  crisping. If the bubbling goes quiet, you overloaded the pot. A karai takes two handfuls at a time,
  no more.
- **Two temperatures, not one.** The low fry cooks the inside and the hot fry crisps the outside. One
  middling temperature gets you neither, and this is the main reason these beat ordinary chips.
- **Reuse the oil.** Cool it, strain it through a sieve, keep it in a sealed jar. It improves over the
  first few uses. Bin it when it smells sharp or foams up.
- **Beef dripping, if you want it.** Render 500g of chorbi over the lowest heat with a splash of water
  for an hour, strain, and blend about 300g into the oil. Made once, keeps a month. Do not fry in pure
  dripping. It sets solid as the chips cool and they turn waxy.

## If you need to cut further

1. Use the blend on frozen chips. Ten minutes total, and still good.
2. Skip the soak and dry the cut potatoes hard instead. Slightly darker, slightly less crisp. Saves
   20 minutes.
3. Skip the blend and use salt and black pepper. Saves nothing on the night, since the blend is made
   ahead, so there is no reason to do it.

## On the nutrition estimate

Oil absorption is the only real variable and it is hard to pin down. A double fry run properly takes
on about 15g of oil per serving. An overloaded pot or oil that has dropped below temperature can
double that, which puts you nearer 450 kcal. This is the same reason the small batches matter for
texture.

The potato itself is about 175 kcal of that. The blend adds almost nothing, around 8 kcal a serving,
and it is mostly salt: roughly 1.5g of salt per serving at 3g of blend.

Frozen chips fried at 190°C come out around 380 kcal a serving, since they are par-fried in oil
before you get them.

Where the brined salt figure comes from: 100g of salt in 2L of brine against about 790g of water
inside 1kg of potatoes puts equilibrium near 3.6g per 100ml, so roughly 28g of salt ends up in the
potatoes if they get all the way there — and over 48 hours a 12mm baton very nearly does. They are
drained but never rinsed, and salt does not cook off. Short of equilibrium it is still most of a
day's allowance in a side dish, which is why that method ends with no extra salt.

The 4-hour fridge brine is a different thing: salt reaches perhaps 3mm into a 6mm half-thickness, so
it seasons the outside and leaves the middle plain. Nearer 2.5g a serving, much closer to the plain
version than to the fermented one.
